 Subject:     BPD Hiring/Staffing Levels and Staffing Level Impact Statements

Purpose
This memorandum provides an overview of the Berkeley Police Department’s current staffing
levels, outlines the projected impacts of the City’s 10% and 12.5% General Fund reduction
scenarios, and explains the operational consequences that would result from implementation.
This analysis reflects current staffing realities, anticipated attrition, recruitment constraints, and
the operational risks associated with eliminating key sworn, dispatcher, and strategic positions
necessary to sustain and modernize police operations.
Department-Wide Staffing Context
Prior to the FY25/26 budget adoption, the Department’s authorized sworn staffing level was
181 positions; the current authorized level is 175. Of those, 154 sworn positions are filled,
including ten officers in the academy and five in field training. The academy cohort includes two
officers who began in October 2025 and are expected to graduate in May 2026, five who began
in January 2026 and are projected to graduate in June/July 2026 (academy dependent), and
three who started in February 2026 and are anticipated to graduate in August 2026. At the
same time, approximately 22 sworn personnel are eligible for retirement by the end of calendar
year 2026, including employees currently on workers’ compensation leave. Routine separations
and lateral transfers continue in an increasingly competitive regional hiring environment.

The Department is a personnel-driven organization, with approximately 93% of its General Fund
budget allocated to personnel costs. As a result, meaningful reductions cannot be achieved
through non-personnel savings alone. While limited non-personnel shifts and overtime
reductions have been identified, they are insufficient to meet either the 10% or 12.5%
reduction targets.

Staffing levels also directly impact overtime expenditures and employee wellness. The 2024
Citygate Workload and Organizational Study1 recommended increasing Patrol staffing from 58
to 73 officers to meet workload demands and reduce overtime reliance. In FY25, minimum
patrol staffing shortages alone accounted for nearly $3 million in overtime costs. Increased
staffing would reduce reliance on overtime and allow officers to take leave and attend training
without creating minimum staffing shortages.

The Department has also made significant financial and operational investments in current
academy recruits, including background investigations, onboarding, academy tuition,
equipment, and staff time dedicated to recruitment and training. Releasing recruits who are
partially through the academy would forfeit these sunk costs, negatively impact morale,
undermine recruitment efforts, and further delay efforts to stabilize staffing.

Hiring and Recruitment Impacts
The Department’s staffing model depends on continuous hiring to offset an average annual
attrition rate of approximately 9.23% over the past decade. At current staffing levels, this
requires hiring approximately 14–16 officers annually simply to maintain existing sworn
strength, before accounting for any reductions. The hiring process averages three to five
months from application to conditional offer and must align with academy start dates
throughout the year. For entry-level officers, the timeline from application to solo patrol
deployment is approximately 18 months.
Because of this extended pipeline, maintaining open and funded positions to hire into is critical.
Even short interruptions in hiring create compounding impacts: separations continue at an
average rate of 9% annually while replacements take up to 18 months to become fully
deployable. Experience from prior hiring freezes demonstrates that once the recruitment
pipeline is disrupted, recovery can take multiple years. Even when funding is restored,
competition from neighboring agencies significantly limits the Department’s ability to rebuild
staffing.
If hiring is frozen as part of budget implementation, routine retirements and separations will
continue without replacement. With a sustained attrition rate near 9%, staffing would decline
annually beyond modeled reduction levels, resulting in sworn staffing below those reflected in
the 10% and 12.5% scenarios and further diminishing operational capacity and response
readiness. A hiring freeze does not stabilize staffing; it accelerates its decline.

Staffing Sustainability and Operational Risk
The Department is operating with minimal staffing buffers. Patrol and the Communications
Center rely heavily on overtime to maintain minimum staffing levels, and routine absences for
training, illness, injury, and leave already strain operations. When combined with an average
9% annual attrition rate, even modest reductions in hiring capacity compound quickly and
reduce deployable staffing year over year.
The COVID-19 period created a sustained disruption to sworn staffing that the Department is
still working to recover from. Beginning in 2020, retirements accelerated, hiring slowed, and
competition for qualified applicants intensified. Between 2019 and 2022, sworn staffing
declined from 169 to 148 officers (a reduction of more than 12 percent) while attrition
continued at historically consistent levels. Because replacing a sworn officer requires
approximately 18 months, losses compounded more quickly than they could be replaced.
This pattern mirrors national and regional trends in which staffing declines coincided with
increased crime, slower response times, reduced proactive policing, and greater reliance on
overtime. Once staffing falls below operational thresholds, recovery requires multiple
consecutive years of sustained hiring and stable funding. Interruptions to hiring, particularly in
the context of a 9% annual attrition rate, risk repeating the prolonged public safety impacts
experienced during the post-COVID staffing crisis.
Further reductions or hiring interruptions would push the Department beyond a sustainable
threshold. As staffing declines, specialty units must be consolidated or eliminated to preserve
patrol response, resulting in reduced investigative capacity, diminished proactive enforcement,
and slower response times. Without sustained hiring, staffing levels will continue to erode
regardless of budget modeling, creating long-term impacts that may take years to reverse.
Communications Center

The Communications Center is currently staffed at 20 of 32 authorized positions and is
responsible for answering emergency and non-emergency calls and dispatching police and fire
resources. Operational capacity is constrained by the limited number of fully trained Public
Safety Dispatcher II personnel, who are the only staff qualified to independently dispatch
emergency calls. Over the past four years, the Center has experienced approximately a 67%
attrition rate among new hires, with most separations occurring during training or within the
first year of employment. As a result, the center relies on mandatory overtime to maintain

Patrol and Specialty Units
Patrol remains the Department’s core function and frontline response to calls for service.
Additional reductions will likely require consolidation or elimination of specialty functions
including Traffic/Motors, Community Services Bureau, the Bicycle Team, and significant
portions of the Detective Bureau to preserve minimum patrol staffing.
Community Services Bureau
The Community Services Bureau serves as the Department’s primary liaison for community
engagement, problem-solving, special event coordination, and collaboration with City
departments on complex quality-of-life issues. The unit plays a central role in addressing long-
term community concerns and supporting the City’s response to homelessness through
sustained partnerships and relationship-based policing. Further reductions would significantly
curtail outreach and coordinated response efforts, shifting complex community issues back to
patrol units already operating with limited capacity.
Bike Team
The Bicycle Team provides focused patrol coverage in business districts and other high-priority
areas while serving as one of the Department’s most flexible deployment resources. The team
supports warrant operations, special events, and patrol backfill, while maintaining a visible
presence and problem-solving approach in commercial corridors such as Downtown and the
South Campus area. Additional reductions would diminish crime reduction and community
engagement efforts and remove a critical, flexible resource that helps stabilize operations
during ongoing staffing shortages.
Traffic Bureau
The Traffic Bureau investigates serious and fatal collisions, conducts traffic enforcement,
manages traffic safety grants, and provides data analysis to support roadway safety initiatives.
Traffic officers possess specialized training required for complex collision investigations and are
integral to Vision Zero initiatives, special event planning, and multi-department coordination on

roadway and encampment operations. Further reductions would shift these responsibilities to
patrol, significantly limiting proactive traffic safety work and reducing overall response capacity.
Detective Bureau
The Detective Bureau conducts follow-up investigations on serious and complex crimes and
manages digital evidence, forensic analysis, and case preparation. Reduction staffing has
already required prioritization among critical cases and limited proactive investigative work,
including cold case review and regional task force participation. Additional reductions would
increase case backlogs, reduce investigative follow-up and victim support, and diminish the
Department’s ability to resolve crimes and maintain effective investigative operations.
Conclusion
The Department is operating at staffing levels with little margin for further reductions.
Retirements and ongoing separations are making it increasingly difficult to sustain specialized
units and maintain minimum service levels.
Any reduction in sworn or dispatcher positions must be weighed against the Department’s
capacity to deliver essential public safety services. Maintaining vacant positions to hire into is
critical; without them, hiring and training delays will create lasting impacts on Berkeley’s public
safety infrastructure and risk replicating the post-COVID staffing crisis experienced by many
agencies, where low staffing led to slower response times, reduced service capacity, and
prolonged recovery.
The modeled 10% and 12.5% reduction scenarios require significant personnel eliminations that
will materially reduce public safety service levels, particularly if hiring is frozen and historical
attrition trends continue.
