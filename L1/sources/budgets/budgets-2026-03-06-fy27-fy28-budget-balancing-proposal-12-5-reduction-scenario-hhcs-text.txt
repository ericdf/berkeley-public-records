Introduction

This memorandum provides key concepts and Director’s thoughts on the Department of
Health, Housing, and Community Services (HHCS) plan to achieve a 12.5% General Fund
reduction for the FY27 and FY28 biennial budget. This memo serves as a high-level
summary; more exhaustive descriptions and justifications for each line item and contract
are maintained within the detailed budget workbook.

Faced with significant fiscal headwinds in both GF and multiple specialty funds, our
department has engaged in a rigorous, principle-driven process to identify $4.4 million in
annual GF adjustments and a variety of other actions to correct specialty fund deficits.
Due to the severity of the structural deficit, the recommendations are deep. While efforts
were made to reduce the impact, the recommendations from HHCS are very painful and
will be met with considerable community concern.

Immediate Challenges with Specialty Funding

1. Aging Services Division, Alameda County Area Agency on Aging (AAA) On March 5th,
AAA provided notification of an impending cut to the Information & Assistance (I&A)
program funding, effective July 1, 2026, totaling $156,803. This program is funded through
the federal Older Americans Act funds that pass through to the Alameda County Area
Agency on Aging (AAA). Berkeley has been a recipient of these funds for well over two
decades. Funds from this program have consistently funded the high-quality social
services that Berkeley provides to the older adult community. The loss of these funds is
devastating to the services that Berkeley provides. Our fiscal team is actively developing a
comprehensive plan to mitigate the effect of this reduction. We are recommending
reallocating Fund 320 funds from a vacant position to support maintaining existing staff on
this grant.

2. California Strengthening Public Health Initiative (CASPHI) Berkeley Public Health
Division also received notification of an impending cut to the program funding, effective
February 11, 2026. While the announcement came out in January, this cut has been the
subject of a federal lawsuit and temporary restraining order. While we don’t have the final
notice, we believe based on input from our state association that the funding will be cut.

3. CalFresh Healthy Living (CFHL) On March 6th, Berkeley Public Health Division received
notification of an impending cut to the CalFresh Healthy Living (CFHL) program funding,
$162,390 annually effective May 1, 2026. Public Health administers a portion of the United
States Department of Agriculture (USDA) funded Supplemental Nutrition Assistance
Program, known in California as CFHL. The purpose of the Grant is to provide allowable
nutrition education and obesity prevention activities and interventions for low-income
Californians. The focus of the project is to upstream public health approaches and
individual and organizational health promotion to help the CFHL target audience establish
healthy eating habits, a physically active lifestyle, and for primary prevention of disease.
The target audience for CFHL is specifically SNAP participants and other low-income
individuals who qualify for SNAP (CalFresh Food) benefits or other means-tested Federal
assistance programs. The program's core activities in Berkeley include direct education for
children aged 0-5, aiming to increase healthy food and water consumption, boost physical
activity, and reduce screen time, with an annual reach of approximately 650 children.
Concurrently, direct education for adults targets teachers, staff, and parents (ages 18-59),
reaching around 300 individuals annually. The program also conducts monthly healthy
food demonstrations for older adults (59+), typically reaching 320 individuals annually, and
provides indirect education through healthy cooking resources at community events.
HHCS has identified alternative one-time funding to continue the program through the
effective date of May 1, 2026. All CFHL services will be discontinued as of May 1, 2026, and
staffing will be redirected to other program areas with alternative available funding for the
remainder of FY26.

5. Impact of Measure GG Revenue Transfer to Fire Department: The Department’s
emergency preparedness capacity will be significantly diminished due to the mandatory
transfer of $339,487 in Measure GG funding to the Fire Department. To accommodate
this loss of revenue, HHCS will be forced to eliminate 2 existing Full-Time Equivalent
(FTE) positions within our preparedness programs. It is important to note that while
these workforce reductions are necessary to balance the departmental budget and are
included in our overall recommendations, they generate zero net savings for the
General Fund or the Department. Instead, this action simply shifts the funding source to
another department, resulting in a direct loss of HHCS staff and a reduction in our ability
to coordinate comprehensive city-wide emergency readiness and response initiatives.

Our Strategic Approach

In navigating these difficult choices, HHCS rejected a "one-size-fits-all" methodology. We
did not consistently utilize across-the-board cuts to contracts because many programs
rely on the General Fund as a critical match for external grants, and others are not General
Fund-supported at all. Instead, we employed a surgical approach rather than an "ax." This
precision allows us to protect leveraged resources, honor our commitment to equity, and
prevent the inadvertent loss of grant revenue.

Guiding Principles

The following principles served as the "north star" for all departmental decision-making:

   •   Financial Health: Prioritizing permanent structural adjustments over one-time fixes
       to ensure long-term stability.
   •   Equity and Access: Maintaining a commitment to meeting the needs of Black and
       Brown residents in underserved communities.
   •   Impact Mitigation: Prioritizing reductions that result in the "least impact" to direct
       community services.
   •   Core Service Protection: Ensuring reductions do not compromise our mandate to
       provide a resilient, safe, and prepared City.



                                             3
   •   Consistency with Public Intent: Honoring the original intent of voter-approved
       taxes (e.g., the Sugar-Sweetened Beverage tax). This will be reduced to zero
       recommended reductions in the next iteration.

Strategies Employed:

   •   Vacancy Management and Reduction in Workforce: Due to projected deficits in
       specialty revenue, the department had been holding positions vacant to avoid
       reductions to filled positions. As a result, the department has a large number of
       vacancies recommended for elimination.
   •   Alternative Funding Swaps: Shifting General Fund (GF) expenditures to Childcare
       Mitigation funds, or existing grant or other funding stream balances (BHSA,
       Realignment, WIC, U1, PLHA, etc.).
   •   Revenue Optimization: Increasing Environmental Health permit fees to align with
       actual costs and neighboring jurisdictions.
   •   Service Realignment: Reducing or eliminating local funding for services that are
       legally the responsibility of Federal, State (Regional Center/Medi-Cal), or County
       systems.
   •   Contract Reductions: A variety of logic frameworks were utilized and detailed in
       the budget workbook to propose reductions depending on the service, impact, and
       funding stream.

4. Mental Health

The Mental Health Division is recommending a reduction of 44 positions. While this
appears to be a large number, it primarily represents a cleanup of vacant positions “on the
books” and "right-sizing" of the division following an ambitious expansion period.
Approximately four years ago, the mental health division scaled up staffing by
approximately 10 new positions in anticipation of CalAIM’s promise of expanded Medi-Cal
populations and increased service needs. However, mid-expansion, several vital changes
occurred to state funding. The State shifted Medi-Cal to a fee-for-service model and
implemented BHSA/Prop 1 changes. As a small jurisdiction acting as a contractor to a
large county for MediCAL, Berkeley was disproportionately penalized by these rate cuts
effectively reducing our Medi-Cal revenue by 50%. In addition, BHSA (Prop 1) diverted
treatment funds toward state-level prevention and housing mandates. Recognizing these
fiscal shifts early on, the Division had the foresight to hold many of these expansion
positions vacant. It’s important to note that over the past four years mental health had an
average of 24 vacant positions. Consequently, only 7 of the 44 proposed reductions are
currently filled. Because many of these roles were never implemented due to the state’s
funding reversals, and adjustments in service due to staffing levels, their elimination will
not result in further loss of established community services. When the City is unable to
provide required MediCAL covered services individuals are referred into the County
system. The only significant impacts to services provided to the community are included in
the Mobile Crisis recommendation.

   •   Mobile Crisis Team: Sadly, the only GF funding left in this division funds 2.6 FTEs.
       While this will be challenged, crisis response is not the responsibility of cities in
       California. Berkeley’s ability to subsidize the County’s responsibility was a luxury of
       surplus funding; however, facing a structural deficit, difficult recommendations
       must be made. The proposed elimination of the Mobile Crisis Team would result in
       the loss of Berkeley’s only directly operated local mental health crisis response,
       shifting the primary responsibility for wellness checks and psychiatric emergencies
       to the Berkeley Police Department. The landscape has also changed significantly
       since we discontinued the Specialized Care Unit, making reliance on external
       providers like Alameda County’s CATT questionable. That program is currently
       facing its own 33% budget reduction and the potential loss of daytime service

Recommendation: Consolidation of Community Health Commission

In alignment with the City Council’s 2021 Systems Alignment initiative and the ongoing
phased approach to Commission Reorganization, I recommend the consolidation of the
Community Health Commission (CHC) into the Health, Life Enrichment, Equity &
Community (HLEEC) Committee as a standing subcommittee. Currently, the CHC
experiences inconsistent meeting schedules and vacancies that delay policy input. By
transitioning the CHC into a Subject Matter Advisory Subcommittee of HLEEC, the City can
maintain resident expertise while ensuring that health policy recommendations are
directly integrated into the Council's workflow, saving significant staff hours within the
HHCS Department.

This proposed change mirrors the Council's previous actions to "right-size" civic
engagement by reducing redundant meeting structures that often result in high vacancy
rates and inconsistent quorums. By transitioning the Commission’s functions to a
specialized advisory subcommittee under the HLEEC, the City can preserve vital resident
expertise while significantly enhancing staff capacity. In response to significant Public
Health funding reductions, this shift will allow Public Health staff to pivot from high-
volume administrative meeting support toward the direct implementation of Council
health priorities, thereby maximizing the impact of General Fund resources during this
stabilization period.

Conclusion

HHCS remains committed to fiscal responsibility while prioritizing the essential needs of
our community. We understand that as fiscal conditions evolve and further analysis is
completed, our strategy may need to shift. We stand ready to pivot our approach, adjust
these recommendations as new revenue data becomes available, or expand upon these
concepts to ensure the City’s long-term stability. We are available to answer any questions
and provide additional details to support City Leadership through these difficult decisions.
