Re: FY27-28 Fire Department Budget Reduction Memo


Due to the number of budget reduction scenarios that cannot be loaded into the
worksheets, we are submitting a separate budget reduction memo to clarify our
proposal. The proposed reductions include reclassifications, reductions to add pays,
and changes to special funds, like Measure GG, that would offset General Fund
expenditures.

In addition to this memo, we have compiled these proposals into a spreadsheet so the
totals can be easily visualized and manipulated. Click here to view that.

Executive Summary
The Fire Department’s FY26 General Fund total revised allocation is $53.58 million; the
non-personnel allocation is $10.4 million. The Department’s personnel budget of $43.1
million makes up 81% of its General Fund allocation. Sworn staff costs make up $40.9
million of the overall personnel budget and are largely dedicated to staff that work on
emergency response apparatus operating from the City’s seven firehouses. Of the 214
total Department FTE, only 13 General Fund–supported positions perform non-
emergency response duties. This profile is the direct result of successive city budget
reductions over multiple decades that prioritized frontline response while permanently
eliminating administrative, prevention, planning, and preparedness FTE and programs.
Given this structure, a 12.5% General Fund reduction will materially degrade the City’s
emergency response capability. These options will have predictable and measurable
negative impacts on the organization’s ability to respond to fires, specialty rescues and
emergency medical incidents within established time standards which will increase
property loss and result in worse patient outcomes. They will further erode our ability to;
conduct core administrative functions like paying employees correctly and managing
grant funds; conduct required annual life-safety inspections and meet the demand for
construction inspections driven by development; sustain disaster readiness; and
manage responder workload – which will impact morale, injury and retention.

Budget Context and Structural Constraints

Over the past three decades, the City has relied on the Fire Department’s strong
community trust to pass three no-sunset parcel tax measures that shifted substantial
fire department costs off the General Fund while expanding services. Concurrently,
General Fund reductions consistently target non-operations functions, resulting in a
department that is operationally efficient but administratively thin.
As a result:
   •   Nearly all remaining General Fund expenditures are personnel-driven and
       operations-critical.
   •   There is minimal discretionary general fund capacity remaining outside of
       emergency operations.
   •   Any further General Fund reductions disproportionately affect preparedness,
       prevention, and response reliability.

Fire Department Tax Measures (FY25 Revenue Summary)

Measure M (2004) – Paramedic Services
Relieved the General Fund of the full cost of 911 Advanced Life Support ambulance
transport and first-responder ALS staffing on all engine companies.

FY25 revenue: $3.95 million
Measure GG (2008)
Adopted following a substantial reduction of General Fund Fire Department overtime
funding which caused temporary, rotating fire station closures. Measure GG relieved the
General Fund of a substantial percentage of minimum staffing overtime costs; funded
fire and police regional interoperable radio participation, disaster preparedness
activities, and First Responder Advanced Life Support (Paramedics) on all emergency
response apparatus.
FY25 revenue: $5.93 million
Measure FF (2020)
Addressed structural deficits that had developed in Measure M and expanded
emergency medical services, training, wildfire preparedness and prevention capacity in
response to increased Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) risk and changing community
hazard profiles.
FY25 revenue: $10.25 million
Collectively, these measures represent an unprecedented effort by the city, the
community and labor to sustain emergency services while substantially reducing
General Fund exposure.

Critically, Measure FF was presented to voters to enhance services and
capabilities, not to replace existing General Fund expenditures. Any FY27–28
budget reduction strategy that eliminates Measure FF–funded positions,
programs or projects that supplants existing General Fund costs with Measure FF
revenues would be inconsistent with the purpose of this tax. Such actions risk
becoming publicly visible through required financial oversight and audit
processes and would erode public trust. This erosion of confidence could have
lasting consequences, including increased scrutiny, exposure during audits, and
diminished voter support for future City revenue measures at the ballot box
including the potential November 2026 Infrastructure Bond and increase to the
Sales Tax.

13. Description: Close Fire Station 4, structural savings through elimination via
    attrition of 3 Firefighter FTE, 3 Apparatus Operator FTE, and 3 Fire Captain
    II FTE
      a. Fiscal Impact: $3,631,106; Ongoing, General Fund
      b. Justification: None
      c. Service/Community Impact: There is no fire station closure that can be
         characterized as low-impact. The proposal to close Station 4 is based
         solely on its relative call volume compared to other engine companies
         1,538, not because its services are less critical to community safety. The
         only station with fewer total responses is Station 7 at Shasta Road and
         Grizzly Peak Boulevard; however, closure of that station would severely
         isolate a geographically constrained area of the City and create an even
         greater operational risk than the closure of Station 4.
         The most reliable way to understand the future impacts of closing Station
         4 is to examine the historical role this unit has played in emergency
         response. In CY2025, Engine 4 responded to 28 fires, 966 emergency
         medical incidents inclusive of 17 cardiac arrests, 13 auto and bike

accidents, four auto vs pedestrian/bike accidents, 79 hazardous
conditions, 230 fire alarms, and 233 other incidents. These responses
represent real emergencies requiring timely intervention to prevent loss of
life, injury, and property damage.
The Fire Department’s total response time performance standard is 510
seconds (8.5 minutes), 90% of the time was only met 83% of the time. The
travel-time performance standard of 300 seconds (5 minutes), 90% of the
time, was met only 73% of the time. This means that for 415 incidents
within District 4 in 2025 it took responders longer than 300 seconds to
travel from the fire station to the emergency location, even with Station 4
in service. Closure of this station will further degrade performance by
forcing responses to originate from more distant stations or from outside
the City through mutual aid agreements with Albany and/or the El Cerrito–
Kensington Fire Department. As a result, the current total response
time performance of 83% and travel time performance of 73% will
substantially degrade.
These delays have direct and measurable consequences. In cardiac
arrest, each minute of delayed defibrillation reduces the likelihood of
survival by approximately 7–10%. Comparable time sensitivity exists for
many medical emergencies routinely handled by the Fire Department,
including respiratory failure, stroke, sepsis, anaphylaxis, and circulatory
shock, where early airway management, electrical therapy, intravenous
access, fluid resuscitation, and medication administration markedly
improve survival and neurological outcomes. Time is equally critical in
structure fires. Modern residential fires can reach flashover in as little as
6–9 minutes, with fire volume doubling every 30–60 seconds. Once
flashover occurs, occupant survivability declines sharply, evacuation
becomes more difficult, and firefighter risk increases significantly. For
traumatic incidents, including street trauma, response times of 12 minutes
or longer are associated with nearly double the fatality rate compared to
responses under 7 minutes.
Beyond individual incidents, closure of Station 4 creates broader system-
wide impacts. The redistribution of its call volume will increase workload at
remaining stations, resulting in longer travel distances, extended unit
commitment times, and reduced recovery periods between calls. Over
time, this decreases available time for employee training and
development, elevates the risk of injury, contributes to fatigue and

         burnout, and reduces the Department’s ability to sustain high-quality
         performance during prolonged incidents or periods of elevated demand.
         First-alarm effectiveness for structure fires will also be reduced. First-due
         assignments are designed to place critical functions (water supply, fire
         attack, search and rescue, and ventilation) on scene within a narrow time
         window. Removing Station 4 increases the likelihood that one or more of
         these functions will be delayed, especially in the Northeast and Central
         Berkeley Hills allowing fires to grow unchecked, worsening interior
         conditions, delaying occupant rescue, and increasing the probability that
         fires extend beyond the room of origin and require additional resources to
         control.
         Closure of Station 4 will also increase reliance on mutual aid for routine
         incidents, not just large or complex emergencies. While mutual aid is an
         essential component of regional response during surges of 911 calls or
         complex incidents, it was not designed for routine dependence. Berkeley
         already calls for mutual aid over 1,000 times per year, increasing this need
         will not be a sustainable solution for the City nor its regional partners.
         Finally, the likelihood of delayed multi-unit responses will increase,
         particularly during peak demand periods. Many fire, rescue, hazmat and
         medical incidents require coordinated responses from multiple units within
         specific timeframes. With Station 4 removed, second-due and third-due
         units will more frequently be responding from greater distances, disrupting
         incident command strategies, slowing escalation when conditions
         deteriorate, and increasing the risk that incidents outpace available
         resources before adequate staffing and equipment arrive.
         In summary, closure of Station 4 will increase response times across its
         service area and adjacent districts, degrade first-alarm effectiveness,
         increase responder workload and fatigue, reduce regional response
         resilience, and worsen outcomes for both medical emergencies and fires
         that occur within the North end of the City. These impacts will be felt daily
         and cannot be fully mitigated through redeployment or mutual aid.
      d. Risk Level: High
      e. Mitigation Plan: Collectively, these impacts would be felt daily and cannot
         be fully mitigated through redeployment or use of mutual aid.
