Department of Information Technology

Fiscal Sustainability and Impact of Proposed FY2027–FY2028 Reductions

Overview

The Department of Information Technology (IT) provides the digital infrastructure that
enables nearly every operational function of the City of Berkeley. These services support
ﬁnancial management systems, permitting and licensing platforms, communications
infrastructure, cybersecurity protections, data management, and the network systems
used by City departments to deliver services to community members.

As part of the City’s broader effort to address a structural ﬁscal imbalance, IT has
evaluated potential expenditure reductions for FY2027 and FY2028. However, the nature of
technology operations creates unique ﬁscal constraints. A signiﬁcant portion of IT
expenditures consists of ﬁxed costs necessary to maintain core systems, enterprise
software licensing, cybersecurity protections, and regulatory compliance requirements
required to sustain City operations.

As a result, reductions to the IT budget must be approached carefully, as they may directly
affect the reliability, security, and long-term sustainability of the City’s technology
infrastructure.



Fiscal Context

The Department’s projected baseline expenditures are approximately $18.6 million in
FY2027 and $18.9 million in FY2028, representing the cost required to maintain the City’s
existing technology infrastructure and services.

Under the current ﬁscal targets, available revenues are projected to be approximately
$15.27 million annually, creating a structural gap between the cost of maintaining existing
services and the available funding.

This gap reﬂects several structural pressures affecting public-sector technology
operations, including:

• Continued increases in enterprise software and cloud service costs
• Increasing cybersecurity and regulatory compliance requirements
• Aging infrastructure requiring lifecycle replacement
• Growing demand for digital services across City departments
These pressures create a challenging environment in which maintaining the City’s existing
technology environment becomes increasingly difficult within the current funding
structure.



IT Funding Structure

Most IT services are funded through Internal Service Fund 680 (IT Cost Allocation). This
model distributes the cost of enterprise technology services across City departments
based on staffing and operational needs.

Although this structure functions as an internal service model, the majority of these costs
ultimately originate from the General Fund, because most City departments rely primarily
on General Fund resources to pay their share of the IT Cost Allocation. As a result, ﬁscal
pressures affecting the General Fund indirectly impact the sustainability of the City’s
technology operations.

The General Fund itself represents approximately 7% of IT’s direct funding, or roughly $1.2
million annually. IT modeled scenarios reﬂecting 10% and 12.5% reductions to this portion
of the budget as requested.

Because the General Fund portion represents a relatively small share of IT’s total funding,
reductions in this area generate limited savings while still affecting the Department’s
operational ﬂexibility.



Historical Fiscal Context

The Department’s current ﬁscal position is also inﬂuenced by prior budget actions. In
FY2026, $6.46 million was removed from the IT Cost Allocation Fund in a one-time action to
balance the FY2026 budget.

Historically, these reserves functioned as a ﬁnancial buffer that allowed IT to manage
infrastructure lifecycle replacements and major system upgrades without disrupting
operations. The removal of these funds signiﬁcantly reduced IT’s ability to absorb cost
increases or respond to emerging technology needs.

As a result, IT entered the current budget cycle with limited ﬁnancial ﬂexibility while
continuing to face increasing operational costs and aging infrastructure.



Actual Liabilities are Greater with Funds 502 and 503 Considered
As reported, the true ﬁnancial liability of the IT department is signiﬁcantly higher. As
discussed in our budget review, the current deﬁcit only accounts for the gap between Fund
680 and the General Fund revenues against projected expenditures. This calculation
overlooks two critical, "sunset" funds that are no longer being replenished but continue to
carry heavy operational costs: VOIP (502) and FUND$ Replacement (503).

   •   Fund 502 (Telephony): Funding ceased at the end of FY2024, yet the fund remains
       responsible for the annual Avaya phone service contract, which costs
       approximately $460K per year. With a projected balance of only $393K by the end of
       FY26, this fund is on a trajectory to reach a negative $551K balance shortly
       thereafter.

   •   Fund 503 (FUND$ Replacement): This fund is in a similarly depleted state and is
       projected to incur a negative balance of $1.8M by the end of FY28.

To ensure ﬁscal transparency and operational continuity, we propose transferring the
liabilities of these two exhausted funds into the 680 Cost Allocation model. While this
move provides a more accurate accounting of our obligations, it will inherently widen the
current deﬁcit gap, necessitating a reevaluation of our total funding requirements.



Cost Pressures in Technology Operations

Technology operations experience cost drivers that differ from other City services.

   •   Vendor Cost Escalation
       Enterprise technology vendors routinely implement annual price increases (average
       12-15%) for licensing, cloud services, and support contracts. These increases are
       typically embedded within renewal agreements and are difficult to avoid.
   •   Regulatory and Security Requirements
       Public sector technology environments must comply with a range of security and
       regulatory standards necessary to protect public data and maintain eligibility for
       cyber insurance and regulatory compliance.
   •   Infrastructure Lifecycle Requirements
       Core infrastructure systems require periodic replacement to maintain reliability and
       performance. Deferring lifecycle investments can increase long-term costs and
       operational risk.

For these reasons, IT’s baseline cost projections represent the minimum level required to
maintain the City’s technology infrastructure.
Proposed Workplan Reductions

To help meet the ﬁscal reduction targets, IT evaluated its planned technology workplan and
identiﬁed projects that could reluctantly be deferred or scaled back.

These proposed reductions total approximately $3.15 million in FY2027 and $2.9 million in
FY2028 and primarily involve postponing infrastructure modernization projects, reducing
support services, and delaying technology initiatives intended to improve operational
efficiency.

The most signiﬁcant reduction involves postponing the City’s telephony integration with
Microsoft Teams, representing approximately $1.6 million in FY2027 and $1.4 million in
FY2028. This project was intended to modernize the City’s aging phone infrastructure and
improve resiliency and reliability. Deferring this project prolongs reliance on aging
equipment and increases the potential risk of service disruptions. The instability of this
unfunded infrastructure has already manifested in critical service gaps. In the past year,
Public Safety Dispatch experienced several Avaya outages that prevented the public from
reaching non-emergency lines and left the Communications Center unable to make
outbound calls. Similar outages at the Transfer Station disabled their ability to process
credit card payments, which rely on a stable, wired phone connection for transactions.

Additional reductions include delaying network and bandwidth upgrades for remote City
facilities, which were originally planned to improve connectivity, reliability, and
performance.

IT also proposes eliminating Service Desk staff augmentation currently provided through
CompuCom, representing approximately $500,000 annually. Removing these resources
however, will reduce Service Desk capacity and will result in longer response times for City
staff requesting technical support.

Other reductions include postponing implementation of an Enterprise Integration Platform,
which was intended to improve coordination between City systems and support
centralized ﬁnancial tools such as an accounts receivable and billing portal. Without this
platform, departments will continue relying on point-to-point integrations, which require
more manual effort and increase system complexity. Another way to look at it: Operating
without an integration platform is like running a city where every department speaks a
different language and we have to hire expensive translators for every single conversation.
An integration platform is the 'Universal Language' that allows our systems to talk to each
other automatically, reducing human error, protecting our data, and ensuring that our IT
personnel spend their time on innovation rather than manual data entry.
Planned investments in Artiﬁcial Intelligence technologies intended to streamline
administrative processes will also be delayed, limiting opportunities to improve operational
efficiency across departments and increasing cyber and privacy risks as personnel seek to
use their own AI solutions.

Finally, the Department proposes eliminating certain vendor support services, including
Microsoft enterprise support, which may increase the time required to resolve complex
technical issues affecting email systems, desktops, and applications.

Ultimately, these proposed workplan reductions delay several modernization efforts and
require the department to operate existing systems longer than originally planned. This
means community members will likely experience a slower, less reliable, and more
fragmented interaction with City staff. By deferring critical infrastructure upgrades and
reducing support capacity, the City risks more frequent service disruptions—similar to the
recent outages that hindered non-emergency public safety lines and paralyzed payment
processing at the Transfer Station.

The consequences of maintaining such vulnerabilities are not theoretical; the 2023
ransomware attack on the City of Oakland serves as a stark warning. In that instance, the
exploitation of aging, underfunded systems led to a months-long state of emergency, the
leaking of sensitive resident data, and the total paralysis of business licensing and permit
processing. By cutting $3.15 million from our own modernization efforts today, we are
effectively choosing to operate on the same fragile foundation that left Oakland vulnerable.
For the general public, this translates to a "hidden tax" of risk: longer wait times for
assistance, a higher probability of systemic collapse during a cyber event, and a digital
infrastructure that continues to age rather than evolve to meet modern needs.




Proposed Workforce Reductions

In addition to reductions within the technology workplan, IT evaluated personnel
reductions to help address the ﬁscal targets.

These reductions total approximately $846,730 in FY2027 and $876,621 in FY2028 in
ongoing personnel savings and focus primarily on vacant positions across multiple
operational areas.

The proposed workforce reductions include:
   •   Application Programmer Analyst II
       This position supports several business systems used by City departments,
       including systems supporting Police and Fire operations. Eliminating this position
       will reduce IT’s ability to respond quickly to system issues and enhancements
       affecting these critical systems.
   •   Customer Service III (311 / Service Desk)
       This role supports the City’s 311 customer service operations. Removing this
       position will reduce call-handling capacity and may result in longer response times
       for community members and City staff requesting assistance.
   •   Project Manager II
       This role provides coordination and oversight for City technology projects.
       Eliminating this position will slow project implementation and reduce oversight for
       technology initiatives requested by City departments.
   •   Senior Information Systems Specialist
       This position supports critical infrastructure systems including servers, network
       systems, and core technology platforms. Eliminating this position reduces IT’s
       technical capacity to maintain and implement infrastructure and security
       improvements.

At face value, one can argue that IT’s head count-to-support ratio of 1:52 (49 IT to 1700-
1800 employees) is “healthy”. In reality, the 1:52 ratio is a mathematical illusion that
misrepresents our operational reality. Most "industry standard" benchmarks assume a
horizontal support structure, where a large pool of interchangeable generalists focuses on
high-volume, low-complexity tasks like PC/laptop "break-ﬁx" support.

In contrast, IT operates as a “vertical shop”, where staffing is dictated by specialized,
mission-critical systems. Of our 49 positions, 22 are dedicated to non-engineering
functions, including public-facing 311 services, internal help-desk support, and essential
ﬁscal/administrative oversight. This leaves a balance of only 27 technical specialists—Sr.
Information Systems Specialists (SISS), Application Programmer Analysts (APA I & II) and
one Program Manager—to manage the City’s critical infrastructure.

These 27 individuals perform the "digital surgery" required to maintain ERMA, FUND$, our
virtual compute environment, custom apps for various departments (Accela, Inspection
Software, HHCS systems) and cybersecurity. Because our structure is vertical, expertise is
deep but narrow; a Programmer Analyst dedicated to applications such as AMCS (Zero
Waste) or berth rentals (PRW) cannot be swapped to troubleshoot a desktop or a phone
line.
In summary, these reductions represent approximately 8% of IT’s overall staffing capacity
and 11% of IT’s technical capacity and reduce the City’s internal technology expertise at a
time when demand for digital services continues to grow.



Strategic Technology Risk: FUND$ Financial System

One of the most signiﬁcant technology risks currently facing the City is the FUND$ ﬁnancial
system.

FUND$ was implemented in 1989 and has been heavily customized over several decades
to support the City’s ﬁnancial operations. While the system continues to function, its age
and customization make it increasingly difficult to maintain and support.

The City currently spends approximately $500,000 per year maintaining the system.
However, because the system relies on outdated technologies and extensive
customization, qualiﬁed support resources are increasingly difficult to obtain.

Given the critical role of this system in managing the City’s ﬁnancial operations, the risk
associated with maintaining the platform continues to grow.

For this reason, replacing the FUND$ system is IT’s primary strategic technology priority for
FY2027 and FY2028. Moving off of this platform will reduce operational risk and improve
integration between ﬁnancial systems and other City applications.



Conclusion

The reductions outlined above allow IT to move toward the City’s ﬁscal targets; however,
they also require deferring key modernization projects, reducing operational support
capacity, and eliminating critical technical positions.

Taken together, these actions shift IT’s focus from technology modernization toward
maintaining the stability of existing systems with fewer resources.

While IT will continue working closely with the City Manager’s Office and the Budget Office
to implement these reductions responsibly, it is important to recognize that sustained
underinvestment in core technology infrastructure will increase operational risk over time.
Aging systems will remain in service longer, modernization efforts will slow, and the City’s
ability to respond quickly to emerging technology needs will be reduced.
Continued investment in the City’s core technology infrastructure—particularly the
replacement of the FUND$ ﬁnancial system—will remain essential to maintaining reliable
City operations and supporting the delivery of services to Berkeley community members.
