Executive Summary
UC Berkeley is consistently ranked as
one of the top universities in the world at
generating innovation and creating
venture-backed startups. It has a broad
array of incubators, accelerators, and
entrepreneurship programs, as well as its
research capacity and other innovation
initiatives from its Innovation and Entrepreneurship (I&E) ecosystem. Guided by the
university’s strategic vision, this ecosystem has created a strong local network effect
between research, entrepreneurship, and industry. The impact of these programs, and
the startups that they produce, help make the campus a strong contributor to the growth
of the Berkeley and Bay Area economies.

UC Berkeley-affiliated startups support more than 500 high-wage jobs in the City of
Berkeley, with an average salary of $216,0001. Each startup job located in the city also
supports an additional 1.05 jobs locally, resulting in a total employment multiplier of
2.052. In total, the activity of these 182 UC Berkeley startups generates more than $300
million in regional value added across Alameda County, and approximately $200 million
in the City of Berkeley alone3.

Qualitative data gathered through stakeholder interviews for this study also highlighted
many positive impacts the campus I&E ecosystem has on the City of Berkeley:

     ➢​ I&E resources draw founders looking to launch or grow their startups in Berkeley.
     ➢​ Successful startups working in campus incubators often first look at office and lab
        space within the city when looking to expand.
     ➢​ The operational needs of I&E startups generate steady economic activity for local
        businesses across a variety of industries.

This data also highlighted a few key growth opportunities for the city and UCB:

     ➢​ Perception of public safety and street conditions contributes to some founders
        hesitating to start or renew commercial leases in Berkeley.

2. METHODOLOGY
This research was scoped to address four questions:

    1.​ How UC Berkeley-affiliated startups interact with the city and regional economy
    2.​ What factors influence their decisions to remain in Berkeley or relocate
    3.​ How the city can better support university innovation while encouraging long-term
        retention
    4.​ The broader impact of UC Berkeley’s innovation and entrepreneurship (I&E)
        ecosystem on the city.

In scope were the jobs, payroll, local spending, taxes, and investment activity of UC
Berkeley-affiliated startups with a city presence, measured through surveys and
administrative data.

Out of scope were startup demographic analysis, broader macroeconomic changes,
long-term startup lifecycle outcomes, housing displacement effects, environmental
externalities, and impacts not attributable to Berkeley-connected ventures or local
expenditures.

Economic impact refers to the measurable economic activity generated in the City of
Berkeley by startups and firms connected to UC Berkeley’s innovation and
entrepreneurship (I&E) programs. This includes direct effects, such as jobs created,
payroll, local vendor spending, commercial space occupancy, tax contributions, and
revenue generated by these ventures. It also includes indirect effects (i.e. supply-chain
spending with local businesses) and induced effects (employee spending in the local
economy). Together, these effects represent the total contribution of UC
Berkeley-affiliated ventures to employment, income, output, and tax revenues within the

local economy. Our study focuses on annual, replicable measurement of direct, indirect,

Economic impacts were estimated using the IMPLAN input-output modeling software.
IMPLAN’s modeling framework is supported by its county-level tax and industry sector
datasets to generate multipliers for each level of impact. It is considered to be the
industry standard for estimating the direct, indirect, and induced effects of economic
activity. See Appendices B-D for details on what was included in the IMPLAN analysis.

Our research approach integrated survey data, qualitative insights, and economic
modeling to assess startup activity and economic contributions to the City of Berkeley.
Data sources included university-affiliated initiatives, the City of Berkeley, startup
surveys, and interviews with founders and program participants.

Entrepreneurial programs were identified through the UC Berkeley BEGIN directory.
Each program was coded by lifecycle stage, participant eligibility, and program type.
This study also identified and surveyed startups that are affiliated with UC Berkeley.

The City of Berkeley Office of Economic Development provided an initial list of
approximately 400 innovation companies operating in Berkeley during Q4 2024. Each
company was reviewed to confirm operational status, activity within Berkeley, and a
clear connection to UC Berkeley. Affiliation was determined through founder ties to UC
Berkeley, participation in university department programs, and engagement with the
broader UC Berkeley I&E ecosystem. Additional outreach to I&E program leaders
ensured completeness, resulting in a final count of 182 startups.

A 14-question survey collected data on employment, vendor spending, fundraising, and
university and city connections. Responses were collected between October 29, 2025
and December 19, 2025. Thirty-four startups responded (22.2%), a voluntary sample
that may not fully represent the full population but provided sufficient data to inform
analysis. Interviews complemented quantitative findings and provided contextual
insights.

Because Berkeley-affiliated startups generate economic value both within the city of
Berkeley, CA, and across the broader Alameda County, impacts are reported separately
for each geographic level to distinguish local impact from regional spillover effects.
Operational impacts were modeled using Berkeley-based employment and spending,
with multiplier effects estimated at the Alameda County level. Capital investment and
tax impacts were modeled separately to maintain conservative, transparent reporting.

BHEP: Helping teams transition into Venture-Focused Programs
Teams that continue beyond early validation often transition into venture-focused

startups progressing toward acceleration and financing.

As ventures mature and require specialized facilities or additional validation, they may

City of Berkeley – Direct Economic Activity9

The 22 startups we surveyed directly support 107 jobs in the city of Berkeley,
generating approximately $23 million in annual payroll, implying an average salary
of roughly $216,000. (A job is defined as full-time equivalent employees working two or
more days per week within city limits.) This compensation level reflects the high-skill,
high-value nature of innovation economy employment associated with UC
Berkeley–affiliated startups.

Survey responses reveal substantial heterogeneity in firm size and economic activity.
This skewed structure is characteristic of startup ecosystems and motivates the
distinction between anchor firms and long-tail firms, rather than reliance on simple
averages. Detailed sample characteristics are provided in Appendix B.2.

Applying the extrapolation framework described in Section 4.3, direct
employment within the City of Berkeley is estimated to range between 516 and
643 jobs, with corresponding annual payroll between $112.3 million and $138.5 million,
and direct value added between $203.6 million and $249.6 million.

5. RESULTS - QUALITATIVE INSIGHTS
Our interviews with founders of UC Berkeley-affiliated startups illuminated consistent
trends on the key positive impacts that both these startups and Berkeley’s I&E
programs have on the city of Berkeley’s economy. The positive reactions provided
context for the high rating of 8.8, that survey respondents gave Berkeley as a place to
grow their startup12. This supports the results produced by the quantitative economic
impact model and reaffirms that I&E activity at UC Berkeley directly materially impacts
Berkeley’s economy. Interviewees also gave suggestions that we grouped into three
opportunities to increase the city of Berkeley’s attractiveness to new startups and
maximize the positive impact of existing startups on the Berkeley community.

After receiving support from UC Berkeley to get their startups off the ground, founders

Qualitative Impacts of Startups Within Berkeley​
Startups that choose to locate in Berkeley boost the local economy in several ways.
They create new, high‑paying jobs, strengthen the local labor market, and increase
employee spending at nearby businesses.

Day‑to‑day operations also support Berkeley vendors. Many campus‑affiliated startups
regularly buy food from local restaurants and rely on nearby suppliers for office
products, printing, cleaning, and facilities services. These recurring purchases provide
stable revenue for small and mid‑sized businesses and reinforce local supply chains.

By leasing offices and laboratories in Berkeley, startups increase demand for
commercial real estate and help sustain specialized facilities like Foundry 31 and
Berkeley Commons. Their presence raises occupancy rates, supports rental income,
grows the property‑tax base, and deepens the city’s innovation ecosystem as firms
scale locally.

UC Berkeley’s innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem itself adds further economic
value. Events hosted by Haas and other campus programs attract visitors who
spend on dining, transportation, and local services, while engaging residents in the
city’s economy. These visitors frequently stay in hotels within Berkeley, and therefore
contribute directly to the city’s revenues by paying the 12 percent transit occupancy tax.

Interview data underline these ties: in FY24–25, about 65% of Bakar Labs’ and 30%
of SkyDeck’s procurement went to Berkeley‑based vendors. Regular spending at
partners such as Noah’s Bagels, Sliver, Safeway, ACC Printers, and Slippery Fish
Catering shows how campus entrepreneurship turns innovation momentum into direct
economic activity in the surrounding community.

While the Bay Area produces many unicorns, startups’ decisions to remain in Berkeley
can be influenced by the surrounding urban environment. One program leader noted
that employee perceptions of safety during commuting, office access, and late work
hours may become a consideration over time. Despite Berkeley’s strong resources and

Streamlining access to regulatory and compliance information, such as through a
Consolidated Regulatory Requirements Document, can improve the ease of doing
business in Berkeley.

For early-stage startups, small fees and administrative requirements can create
meaningful burdens. Founders reported spending significant time navigating Berkeley’s
licensing and permitting processes, and leaders from some programs noted that even
modest fees or procedural complexity can influence location decisions. We believe
these challenges relate more to administrative friction—such as timing and process
complexity—than to the regulations themselves.

Startups also cited difficulty identifying and navigating compliance requirements,
particularly during early formation. A consolidated digital portal outlining permitting,
licensing, and regulatory processes could reduce uncertainty, especially for
laboratory-based startups facing hazardous waste requirements.

6. CONCLUSIONS & CONSIDERATIONS
While it may seem intuitive that UC Berkeley is a main driver of innovation and
entrepreneurship in the City of Berkeley, this report is the first of its kind to assess the
impact of the campus’s I&E and affiliated startups on the city. In our study, we identified
the city-located startups that were affiliated with UC Berkeley and evaluated the
ecosystem of campus I&E programs that support them from ideation to revenue
generation. We then used surveys and subsequent analysis via IMPLAN to estimate the
economic impact of startup activity on the City of Berkeley. Our results indicate that this
activity has created 516 jobs and driven slightly more than $200 million of added
value to the city’s economy.

Moreover, conversations with startup founders, their employees, and leaders of campus
I&E programs confirmed that UC Berkeley’s innovation activity positively impacts the
city in multiple ways:
    ➢​ Startups and I&E programs contribute directly to the local economy by
        paying for the services of local restaurants, printing companies, and facility
        maintenance professionals.
    ➢​ Existing startups, along with their employees and funding, are drawn to the
        Berkeley area because of the entrepreneurial and R&D resources that are
        available on campus.
    ➢​ Founders of successful on-campus startups often first look at office and lab
        space within the City of Berkeley because of their familiarity with the area,
        supporting the city’s commercial real estate market.
    ➢​ The university connects the city to a large network of skilled professionals
        and researchers, enhancing the reputation of Berkeley as an innovative hub and
        drawing startups to Berkeley.
    ➢​ Large conferences and events held by I&E programs draw in visitors who
        support local restaurants and contribute to the city’s tax revenue through the 12
        percent transit occupancy tax.


Considerations for the City of Berkeley
Our qualitative research unearthed several growth opportunities that we have
condensed into four key recommendations for the City of Berkeley to consider
incorporating into its innovation initiatives.

​      ​      ​      ​      ​      ​      UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


    ➢​ Improve perception of public safety: While the City of Berkeley has made
       great strides in the last two years in reducing crime, in particular with commercial
       burglaries decreasing significantly for the second straight year, many founders
       we interviewed who left Berkeley cited safety concerns around downtown.
       Continuing to make substantive progress on crime while also publicizing this
       success could help to retain startups in the city.

    ➢​ Partner with UCB to spread awareness of philanthropic opportunities: Many
       UC Berkeley affiliated startups expressed interest in giving back to the
       community but were unsure how to do so. Coordinating with campus I&E leaders
       to promote programs such as the Berkeley Startup Cluster’s Berkeley Ventures,
       Berkeley Values program could substantially increase community involvement.

    ➢​ Streamline regulatory processes and information: A common difficulty
       encountered by startups was finding relevant regulatory information to ensure
       they were compliant with City of Berkeley policies. Improved visibility and direct
       distribution of existing resources, such as the business license registration guide,
       would increase awareness of relevant regulations among startups. Additionally,
       creating a centralized online portal for permit and business license applications
       would further ease compliance, reduce administrative burden, and potentially
       improve startup retention and revenue for the city.

    ➢​ Increase accessible short-term lease office and lab space: Actively seeking
       out and incentivizing commercial spaces that are smaller and easily customizable
       would directly address a challenge faced by many startups who struggle to find
       affordable lab and office space that meets their technical needs. Current options
       like the Berkeley Commons are too large and require too much time to customize
       for startups that are time and funding constrained.

Considerations for UC Berkeley
UC Berkeley should also encourage startups from all industries to become involved in
the I&E ecosystem on campus, but in particular promote supportive efforts for biotech
and software through campus resources and programs.


    ➢​ Invest in R&D: Strong areas for the innovation ecosystem leaders to invest in
       include R&D, both through the university and in the surrounding areas focused
       on climate, biotech, medtech, and applied research technologies to expand the
       industrial base that the UC Berkeley-affiliated startups are nurturing in the city.

    ➢​ Invest in short-term lease rental space for startups organized by Berkeley
       professors, students, and network: To foster entrepreneurship and a

Appendix B: IMPLAN Modeling Framework

B.1 Modeling Objective

IMPLAN was used to estimate the economic impacts generated by Berkeley-affiliated
startups through employment, supplier spending, and household consumption effects.
The analysis focuses on operational activity and reports impacts as the combined effect
of direct, indirect, and induced channels.

Because survey responses were obtained from 34 firms out of an identified ecosystem
of 182 startups, modeled results represent verified observed impacts that later serve as
the empirical foundation for ecosystem extrapolation.

B.2 Data Preparation

Survey responses were cleaned and standardized prior to modeling. The following
variables were extracted for IMPLAN input:

    ●​ Berkeley FTE: Employees working two or more days per week in Berkeley,
       modeled as full-time equivalent employment.
    ●​ Vendor spending: Ongoing operational expenditures, modeled as Industry
       Output.
    ●​ IMPLAN sector assignment: Each firm was manually assigned to an IMPLAN
       sector based on primary business activity.


                                                                                         21
​            ​     ​         ​         ​    ​         UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


      ●​ Tax compliance status: Presence or absence of a Berkeley business license.

Firms with no verified local operating presence were excluded. Duplicate survey entries
were consolidated. An internal sector crosswalk was maintained to ensure transparency
and replicability, recognizing that many startups span hybrid software, research, and
professional service activities.

B.3 Firm Inclusion Rules

Each firm was classified into one of three modeling categories:

    ●​ Include (Employment + Output): Firms reporting Berkeley-based FTE and
       vendor spending (17 firms). Modeled using both Industry Employment and
       Industry Output events.
    ●​ Output-only: Firms without Berkeley FTE but with verified local vendor spending
       or lab/office activity (5 firms). Modeled using Industry Output only.
    ●​ Exclude: Firms reporting no local employment, no identifiable local operational
       or capital activity, or inconsistent survey responses (12 firms). Not modeled.

This classification avoids double counting and ensures that impacts reflect verified
Berkeley-based economic activity.

B.4 IMPLAN Event Structure

Two operational IMPLAN event types were applied:

    ●​ Industry Employment: Berkeley-based workers.
    ●​ Industry Output: Ongoing vendor spending.

Vendor spending followed IMPLAN’s default regional purchasing assumptions. Because
early-stage startups often procure specialized services outside the immediate region,
indirect effects may be modestly overstated. This limitation is addressed in the
interpretation section and motivates future sensitivity analysis.

B.5 Geography and Commuting

IMPLAN modeling was conducted at the Alameda County geographic level due to
dataset resolution constraints. Although employment inputs were localized to
Berkeley-based workers, induced household spending and supplier linkages were
allocated across the broader Alameda County regional economy.

Accordingly, induced effects should be interpreted as regional spillovers generated by
Berkeley-based startup activity rather than spending confined strictly within city
boundaries.

B.8 Interpretation of Observed Results​
IMPLAN results demonstrate that even a subset of Berkeley-affiliated startups

     ●​ Non-responding firms,

Appendix C: Extrapolation Framework

C.1 Purpose

Because only a subset of Berkeley-affiliated startups provided usable survey data, an
extrapolation framework was developed to approximate ecosystem-wide economic
impacts. The objective is not to produce a census estimate, but to generate a
defensible, evidence-based impact range grounded in observed data and transparent
assumptions.



                                                                                                             26
​      ​       ​       ​        ​      ​    UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


C.2 Conceptual Structure

Startup ecosystems do not follow linear or normal distributions. Empirical evidence
consistently shows that innovation systems exhibit heavy-tailed behavior, where a small
number of firms account for a disproportionate share of employment, output, and value
creation.

To reflect this structure, the extrapolation framework adopts a two-segment model:

    ●​ Anchor firms: large, high-employment startups treated as directly observed.
    ●​ Long-tail firms: the broader population of smaller startups representing the
       typical ecosystem participant.

Accordingly, the methodology:

    1.​ Treats anchor firms as directly observed and not subject to scaling.
    2.​ Models the remaining firms as a long tail.
    3.​ Scales only the long tail to the full ecosystem.
    4.​ Recombines anchors and extrapolated tail to estimate total ecosystem impacts.

This structure preserves the empirical distribution of firm sizes and avoids distortions
caused by linear averaging.

C.8 Statistical Estimation of Missing Top Firms

Dataset Preparation

Firm-level Berkeley employment was extracted for all startups with positive FTE. Firms
with zero FTE were excluded from the statistical fit.

Lognormal Transformation

Employment values were transformed: LN_FTE= ln(FTE_inBerkeley)

Distribution parameters were estimated:

    ●​ μ=mean(LN_FTE)
    ●​ σ=std_dev(LN_FTE)

Given the limited number of observations, formal goodness-of-fit tests lack sufficient
power to reliably validate the distributional assumption. Therefore, the lognormal
specification is adopted based on well-established empirical evidence in the literature
on firm size and startup growth patterns (See Appendix B.2).

Monte Carlo Simulation

Using the fitted lognormal distribution, Monte Carlo simulation generated synthetic firm
employment values for a population of 182 startups.

Each iteration:

    ●​ Generated 182 firms,
    ●​ Ranked firms by employment,
    ●​ Recorded anchor-scale firms (≥20 FTE),
    ●​ Recorded top 3-10 firm sizes.

A total of 300 iterations were performed.

Appendix D: Key Definitions
The economic impact model outlined below uses the terms set by IMPLAN, and in order
to correctly interpret the outputs, it is necessary to understand the exact meaning and
implications of the terms Value Added and Output. The definitions for both of these
terms as described by IMPLAN are outlined below16.

D.1 Value Added
The total market value of all final goods and services produced within a region in a
given year. Value Added is also a measure of an Industry's production, but with the
costs of Intermediate Inputs removed. Value Added represents the incomes that are
generated by production. Value Added can be measured in two different ways, both of
which will produce the same results. It can either be measured by subtracting the cost
of Intermediate Inputs from an Industry's total Output or by summing each Industry's
payments to the components of Value Added: Labor Income (LI), Other Property
Income (OPI), and (TOPI). In effect, Value Added represents the contribution of the
Industry to the economy.

D.2 Output
A measure of the total value of all goods produced. Value Added is a subset of Output
and is a useful measure of wealth created by an economy. An Industry buys goods and
services from other Industries and remanufactures those goods and services to create a
product of greater value (Output) than the sum of the goods that goes into its product
(Intermediate Inputs). That increase in value is the value that the producer adds to the
inputs as a result of the production process. This added value is then used to pay labor
and taxes with hopefully some remainder for profit.

Appendix E: Tax Impact Interpretation and Limitations

E.1 Nature of IMPLAN Tax Estimates​
IMPLAN tax outputs represent modeled tax incidence, not verified tax collections. Tax
estimates are calculated using sector-average effective tax rates applied to modeled
labor income, production, and consumption flows. As a result, IMPLAN tax results
reflect the distribution of tax burdens across levels of government rather than actual
payments made by individual firms.

Tax categories reported by IMPLAN include:

    ●​ Sub-County General
    ●​ Sub-County Special Districts
    ●​ County
    ●​ State
    ●​ Federal

These categories combine multiple tax instruments such as sales and excise taxes,
property-related taxes, income taxes, payroll-related taxes, and selected
business-related fees.

Accordingly, IMPLAN tax results should not be interpreted as direct fiscal revenues
collected by any specific jurisdiction.

E.2 City of Berkeley Interpretation​
This modeling includes all 22 firms with verifiable Berkeley economic activity regardless
of business license status. This approach captures the fiscal incidence associated with
actual economic activity, not verified tax compliance.

The tax estimates below reflect modeled tax incidence based on economic activity
(employment, payroll, spending). They should be interpreted as:

    ●​ Potential fiscal contribution if all firms were compliant.
    ●​ Economic activity scale for policy planning purposes.
    ●​ NOT actual verified tax payments to the City of Berkeley.

Because only 2 of 22 modeled firms had verified business licenses, actual municipal tax
collections are likely substantially lower than modeled estimates.

Methodological Choice: All Economic Activity vs. Verified Compliance
This appendix reports tax incidence based on Scenario B (All Firms) to capture the
fiscal scale associated with observed economic activity. However, it is critical to
distinguish:
    ●​ Modeled tax incidence: $228K direct (all 22 firms with economic activity).
    ●​ Verified compliant contribution: ~$112K direct (2 firms with licenses).
    ●​ Non-verified activity: ~$116K direct (20 firms without verified licenses).

The $116K difference should NOT be interpreted as:
   ●​ Unpaid taxes or tax evasion.
   ●​ Recoverable municipal revenue.
   ●​ Evidence of intentional non-compliance.​

It may reflect:
    ●​ Early-stage firms not yet meeting licensing thresholds.
    ●​ Administrative delays in license processing.
    ●​ Firms operating remotely with only UC Berkeley affiliation.
    ●​ Legitimate exemptions from certain license requirements.
    ●​ Actual non-compliance (unknown proportion)

For fiscal planning and budgeting, the verified compliant figure ($112K) represents the
most conservative estimate. For economic development analysis, the total economic
activity figure ($228K) provides a more complete picture of startup ecosystem fiscal
footprint.

E.5 Interpretation Guidance​
IMPLAN tax estimates should be interpreted as:
      ●​ Indicators of fiscal scale associated with startup economic activity,
      ●​ Not as direct measures of municipal tax collections,
      ●​ Not as evidence of tax non-compliance or underpayment by specific firms.

For Berkeley specifically:
   ●​ Use: Direct Sub-County General.
   ●​ Caution: Total Sub-County General (~$509K observed) - includes regional
      spillovers.
   ●​ Avoid: Total tax incidence (~$11.6M) - primarily state/federal.

    Future work should compare IMPLAN modeled tax incidence with:
      ●​ City of Berkeley business license revenue (actual collections).
      ●​ Sales tax collections (city share) - Proportional allocation of county and state tax
          streams.
      ●​ Property tax receipts from startup-occupied commercial space.

Such triangulation would improve fiscal attribution accuracy and allow validation of
IMPLAN sectoral assumptions against Berkeley-specific data.




Appendix F: Limitations and Needs
This analysis provides a robust evidence-based estimate of startup economic impacts,
but several limitations should be acknowledged:


                                                                                            40
​      ​      ​      ​      ​      ​       UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


Methodological Limitations

    1.​ Geographic Resolution: IMPLAN's smallest available geography is Alameda
        County, preventing direct modeling of multiplier effects at the Berkeley city level.
        As a result:
    ●​ City-level results are limited to direct activity only.
    ●​ Spillover effects (indirect/induced) are modeled regionally.
    ●​ True city-retained economic activity is likely between direct-only and total
        regional estimates.
    2.​ Tax Incidence Modeling: IMPLAN tax results represent modeled sectoral
        averages and cannot be interpreted as actual City of Berkeley revenue
        collections. Firm-level tax compliance varies, and only 2 of 22 modeled firms had
        verified business licenses at the time of analysis. Tax estimates (Appendix E)
        should be viewed as indicative only.

Data Limitations

    1.​ Survey Coverage: Only 34 of 182 identified firms (19%) provided usable survey
        responses, limiting precision. While extrapolation scenarios address this gap,
        residual uncertainty remains regarding:
    ●​ Non-response bias (are respondents representative?).
    ●​ Accuracy of self-reported data.
    ●​ Ecosystem boundary definition (who qualifies as "Berkeley-affiliated"?)


    2.​ Firm Churn: Startup ecosystems exhibit high entry/exit rates. This analysis
        provides a point-in-time snapshot but cannot account for:
    ●​ Startups founded after data collection.
    ●​ Firms that ceased operations during the study period.
    ●​ Rapid growth/decline between survey and analysis.




Appendix G: Methodology Learnings

Methodology Learnings
On the data collection side of the project, attending events is vital to having the
necessary components of who startups are. This is where the heart of the project
exists— in the conversations with founders on their experience and hopes for the city of
Berkeley. Using a multiple-person approach of interviewing startups to gather
information and clarify ambiguities throughout each week is a necessary implementation


                                                                                          41
​      ​      ​       ​      ​      ​       UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


that our team discovered later in the process. Targeting different themes and directions
of interviews is important. When analyzing responses, one should consider numerical
results alongside personal accounts, integrating empirical evidence with interpretive
understanding to guarantee a well-rounded perspective into the lives of startups.

Potential Solutions:
Through the research process, the research team encountered technological, timeline,
and storytelling challenges. By effectively identifying holes in the study, potential future
iterations may use the following suggestions for more efficient, thorough, and accurate
results.

    a.​ Build a digital portal​
        Through our own process, we determined that building a digital portal to integrate
        entrepreneurial resources within the city’s governmental approvals would be
        beneficial, as well as a digital identity to track projects throughout their process of
        moving to the city. Additionally, observing the amount of federal taxes paid by a
        company that reenters Berkeley’s economy in the form of federal grants is a
        promising project that could be completed in more time. Berkeley-based vendor
        and operating expenditures constitute the primary operational inputs for IMPLAN
        employment and output events. This project could be led by researchers
        collecting this data.


    b.​ Survey Design and Distribution
        Through the survey process, we found that startups were less likely to respond
        when the survey appeared lengthy. To maximize response rates, the survey was
        limited to 10 clearly worded questions to keep it approachable. On the
        distribution side, framing the research as aiming to help the I&E ecosystem for
        startups can improve response quality. Cold outreach yielded limited results,
        while responses were strongest when departments leveraged existing
        relationships to invite participation.

    c.​ Interviewing startups
        On the data collection side, attending events proved essential to understanding
        who the startups are and how they operate. The core of the project lies in direct
        conversations with founders. This is where the heart of the project exists— in the
        conversations with founders on their experience and hopes for the city of
        Berkeley. Using a multiple-person approach of interviewing startups to gather
        information and clarify ambiguities throughout each week is a necessary
        implementation that our team discovered later in the process. Targeting different



                                                                                            42
​      ​      ​      ​      ​      ​      UC Berkeley Institute for Business Innovation


       themes and directions of interviews is important. When analyzing responses, one
       should consider numerical results alongside personal accounts, integrating
       empirical evidence with interpretive understanding to guarantee a well-rounded
       perspective into the lives of startups.

    d.​ Alternative approaches to IMPLAN
        We discovered that using the economic software IMPLAN was limiting for our
        research scope, as it can examine at the county level but not the city level. This
        was extremely limiting, and urged us to consider whether there is another way to
        measure independent economic impact of cities rather than the analysis of
        counties for future studies? Evaluating the economic impact of the startups on
        the city of Berkeley rather than Alameda County would be more accurate.
        Additionally, because IMPLAN tax results represented modeled tax incidence
        rather than municipal collections, future studies should seek to collect firm-level
        data on business license payments, local sales tax generation, and other
        Berkeley-specific tax streams for accuracy. Increased survey coverage and
        repeating data collection would allow for more precise results given more time
        and data. Lastly, given the heavy-tailed nature of startup ecosystems, we should
        separate anchor firms from the long-tail population, extrapolating only the tail
        while preserving observed anchor impacts. A Monte Carlo simulation based on
        firm-level FTE distributions provides a defensible approach to estimating the
        presence and size of unobserved high-performing firms.
