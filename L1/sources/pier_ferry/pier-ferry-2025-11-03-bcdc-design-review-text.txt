  4. Berkeley Waterfront and Ferry Pier Review. The Berkeley Waterfront and Ferry Pier
     project proposes an electric ferry service located at the Berkeley Marina, in the City of
     Berkeley, Alameda County. The project proposes demolition of the closed fishing pier
     and the construction of a new 1,080-foot-long public pier with pedestrian access and a
     ferry terminal, connected to a 400-foot-long breakwater. The proposal also includes
     improvements to the surrounding Berkeley Waterfront, including a public plaza,
     improved site circulation, a Bay Trail extension, parking lot renovations, and new
     landscaping.

       Summary of Key Issues and Board Comments

       Overall Project Configuration

          (1) Board member Pellegrini stated that he has questions related to the needs of the
              recreation users and the access they require, especially given the public concern
              about recreational access as it relates to long standing availability of parking at
              this location. He noted he doesn’t see issues related to physical public access
              based on the project design presented thus far and also noted that while free
              parking is a welcome asset, many public places in the Bay Area find that the way
              to maximize access can’t be done through the delivery of free parking.

          (2) Board member Pellegrini noted that the edge condition for this area of the marina
              doesn’t encourage people-centered activity along the shoreline. He noted the
              existing condition has a lot of passive open space, limited site lines and urbane
              public access unlike other active and programmed waterfronts, such as Treasure
              Island. He continued that the existing pier is not usable and accessible from the
              approach, noting that the lawn and promenades are on the perimeter of parking.
              He felt that the new plaza design will enhance the existing level of public access
              but questioned if it is the best approach to maximize usability.

          (3) Board member Pellegrini noted that shifting the shoreline road could create larger
              amounts of open space and public access, and a reconfiguration of the program
              activities could take place in this location. He highlighted that waterfronts are
              transitioning from suburban style-restaurant uses to uses that are less reliant on
              car travel. He also noted that the triangle is “left over” space and could be
              reconfigured to create additional open space opportunities.

          (4) Chair McCann asked Board member Pellegrini to expand on the idea of road
              reconfiguration to create more shoreline space recreation. She raised concern
              that a reconfiguration may create less parking, for example. Board member
              Pellegrini responded that there is so much extra space, it's not clear that those
              two uses are exclusive. He highlighted the berming of the site and the issues with
              site lines, and encouraged rethinking the landscape. He noted that the space
              could be more than accommodating people waiting for the ferry; a greater
              opportunity for seating or lawn space to picnic to draw people here to participate
              in new activities that didn’t historically happen is possible. He noted that this area
              is based on a suburban model that is car reliant and this project is an opportunity
              to rethink that model and allow for new uses. Improving bus access and bike
              access will create a new opportunity for people to use this area in different way.

Dear Board Members:

I submit this letter to be entered into communications for the November 3 meeting of the Design
Review Board.

I believe that you, the BCDC, have jurisdiction over development of the Berkeley Marina, where
plans are underway to install infrastructure to start charging for parking in key parking lots on
January 1, 2026.

Many residents, waterfront users, and consultants have testified that charging for parking will
cause more harm than good. The arguments have been raised that it will burden the
lowest-income visitors, hinder affordable participation in waterfront nonprofit organizations,
impede access to water sports, cause difficulties downstream for berthers, and cost more money
than it generates, yet the plan pushes on. The desire for a quick budget fix is said to justify
monetizing the waterfront that has, for a hundred years of its existence, been a freely accessible
public resource for the people of Berkeley and the East Bay.

I don’t want to speak to those concerns, valid though they are, but to the very idea of a free
waterfront, which is a resource in itself. Being an intangible and spiritual resource, this is likely
to be overlooked in the calculations of what value can be eked out of the Berkeley Marina’s real
estate.

When a place like the Berkeley waterfront remains open and freely accessible, it serves as a quiet
equalizer in an increasingly unequal world. It offers a shared horizon, one that asks nothing of us
except that we show up: to walk, to breathe, to look out across the Bay and feel, for a moment,
that we belong to something larger than ourselves. To begin attaching a price to that experience,
even in the form of parking fees, is to begin redefining the space from a commons into a
commodity, and to send the message that access to nature and beauty depends on one’s ability to
pay.

In essence, a free public waterfront is not just a stretch of land by the water; it is a public trust, a
symbol of collective belonging, and a source of meaning that cannot be measured in dollars. I
felt this personally when I left my home in Minnesota and drove 2,000 miles west, with only my
dog and a vague plan to settle somewhere in the Bay Area. My search ended almost as soon as it
began. My first stop after Truckee was the South Cove East Lot, next to Roaming Bean Coffee
and Cal Adventures. The moment I stepped out and saw the shimmering ocean, the San
Francisco skyline, the flowers in bloom, and the windsurfers and sailors zipping around, I knew I
had found my place. Within months, I purchased a 30-foot sailboat and made the Berkeley
Marina my home, “where the heart is”: the place where I sail, work on my boat, and find
restoration almost every day. And whenever the projects and stresses of daily life begin to pile
up, I return to that same spot by Roaming Bean and dive in.

Thus, when I think about what it means to preserve free public access to the Berkeley waterfront,
I return to the words of Wallace Stegner in his 1960 letter to the University of California’s
Wildland Research Center in Berkeley. He described how we need wilderness in places like these
not just for the usefulness they provide, but because they offer something deeper, something that
steadies the human spirit:

“These are some of the things [the waterfront] can do for us. That is the reason we need to put
into effect, for its preservation, some other principle than the principles of exploitation or
‘usefulness’ or even recreation. We simply need that wild [expanse] available to us, even if we
never do more than drive to its edge and look in. For it can be a means of reassuring ourselves of
our sanity as creatures, a part of the geography of hope.”

The waterfront’s value to the spirit of Berkeley is immeasurable. I urge you to step in to keep it
free and accessible to all.

Sincerely,
Sarah Lindsey Anderson

In the June 24, 2025 meeting, the Berkeley City Council adopted a resolution to install
pay stations in selected parking lots of the south waterfront of the Berkeley marina.
Council voted unanimously despite a large public response against the plan in the short
lead-up between announcement and council vote. We can provide communications
from at least 540 Berkeley residents as part of the public record. The stated rationale
was revenue generation to relieve the Marina Fund. Evidence does little to support this
statement. Parking management in Berkeley is usually at best revenue neutral and is
primarily for demand management. The documents provided to Council for this parking
proposal did not include costs until the last hours before the council meeting (see
presentation), did not include enforcement costs in later documents and therefore
overstate net revenue. Earlier city estimates of general parking in the marina should a
ferry plan be adopted show zero net revenues. The concern we raise goes far beyond just
a fear of “losing free parking,” as city staff have characterized the opposition in public
meetings, to issues of practicality and access to the shoreline for visitors of all
socioeconomic backgrounds.

stations, which we argue would discourage equitable access to the coastline in
contradiction to BCDC’s mission to assure coastline access in the Bay Plan, Part IV. The
inequity would be most palpable within the exact lots proposed for the pay stations.

                                Overview of concerns

The Berkeley marina has long been a waterfront agora and mecca for city residents and
visitors seeking to use and enjoy its singular mixture of natural and human resources. It
draws a range of people of widely divergent economic means from Berkeley,
neighboring cities, and afar. All seek respite and recreation options in this local and
regional gem. Projects proposing major changes to the area’s physical and social
landscape must undergo rigorous debate about impacts, costs, and benefits affecting
both built structures and the cultural and human environment. In view of this, the EIR
must thoroughly and rigorously assess the physical, cultural and human environmental
impacts of the proposed new Berkeley pier and ferry service, expected to serve around
915 passengers departing per day (about 0.7% of the Bay Bridge vehicular traffic),
with its attendant facilities, including parking. In particular, the scope of the EIR must
characterize the individual and cumulative effects of a dual construction (pier plus ferry
facilities) on equitable access by the public, capital infrastructure and ongoing costs for
Berkeley/Alameda County residents, constriction of the marina’s limited parking
options, impacts on sustainable fishing and recreational boating, other environmental

   1. Impacts on social equity and cultural resources
The Berkeley Pier has long standing cultural significance for Berkeley. Since it was
closed to ferries in 1937, it has been a focal point for recreation and an important, low-
cost asset for many visitors. The Berkeley Fishing Memories Oral History Project
documents intergenerational and community connections to the outdoors as
experienced at the pier and waterfront by Berkleyans and frequent visitors. It reveals
how people have experienced and appreciated the recreational pier and waterfront as
“Berkeley’s backyard,” connoting a low-cost, welcoming, relaxed atmosphere where
children and adults enjoy the outdoors in a relatively safe space, for almost 90 years.
This use of this space is historical and current. For Berkeley, Alameda County, and
beyond, the pier and Berkeley Marina area are integral to low-cost public access to
bluespace. Among other data, the Kittleson parking study (p. 42) across lots in the
waterfront area underscores the socioeconomic diversity of current Berkeley waterfront
visitors. Forty-seven percent reported earning below the median household income for
Berkeley of $108,558
(https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/berkeleycitycalifornia/PST045224).




                                                                                             2
Science increasingly shows the positive impacts of regular bluespace exposure on
mental and physical health (Gascon et al. 2017, Pouso et al. 2021, Sturm and Cohen
2014, While et al. 2020). As the only urban shoreline in Berkeley, it provides a crucial,
inclusive center for public recreation, relaxation, interaction, and growth through
multiple educational and sport-related resources located at the Marina. Further, it
provides these benefits to historically underserved communities and low- and middle-
income users who have fewer means to travel to other coastal areas for shoreline
access.

While the NOP notes maintaining public space access as an objective, related COB and
WETA project documents and considerable COB Parks Department staff time has
focused on the needs of the proposed ferry service as the primary objective, with
recreation secondary. For example, during the course of WETA ferry project
negotiations, funds earmarked for waterfront and marina infrastructure have been
diverted to the ferry project at the expense of maintaining existing recreational
amenities. Further, basic, long-standing facilities central to the waterfront and marina’s
success as a bluespace public good – e.g. the failing seawall along the Bay Trail bike
path in front of the area’s two sailing clubs and docks, the recreational east dock in the
South Cove, and the watersports launch at Hs Lordship’s cove – have been allowed to
deteriorate and/or be shuttered. As of May 1, 2025, the COB has laid off its waterfront
security monitors, making recreational usage less safe and secure. These monitors
cost $100,000/year.

These points also illustrate overlaps in project scope between the ferry project and the
WSP. The spatial, policy and operational requirements of the ferry service impede
affordable recreational opportunities, as noted in the parking studies, as would a WSP
design focus to attract mainly ferry patrons. Data shows that riders in the WETA ferry
system tend to be high-income. Serving the high-income segment of population while
lessening services to others will transform the waterfront’s value as a cultural and
affordable public bluespace recreational resource into a transportation hub supporting
mainly a high-income ridership profile.

   2. Baseline comparison must be recreational demand at waterfront with
      pier and without ferry

City staff have indicated that the baseline for comparison is the status today of the
waterfront area and pier. However, COB closed the pier in 2015 after a long history of
popularity and high usage. Subsequent to closure, the city has made no new capital
investments to maintain walkways, seawalls, or adjacent parking lots around the
shoreline or pier. Around 2017, COB began negotiations with WETA for a ferry terminal,
reviving talks reflecting a 2008 proposal to place a WETA ferry terminal at the location.
Hs Lordships Restaurant closed in 2018 and remains derelict. D&E docks have been
removed. While there is still extensive usage of the waterfront despite 10 years of
minimal maintenance, the feasible baseline activity should be usage of a reopened
pier (to 2000’ or 3000’) on par with its length prior to closure, not baseline usage
after 10 years of the waterfront area falling into disrepair during COB-WETA
negotiations.

Research and reports indicate a large increase in public demand if the pier were
reopened and a basic functional facility maintained:
   ● Ken Jones, chronicler of pier fishing in California, states: “If a survey had been
      done over the past twenty-five years as to the most visited pier in the Bay Area,
      the winner undoubtedly would have been either the pier at Pacifica or this pier in
      Berkeley” and “For availability, transportation, ease of fishing, and facilities,
      Berkeley was hard to beat” (https://www.pierfishing.com/berkeley-pier/).
   ● Before its closure, Hs Lordships Restaurant was heavily used as an event space
      for large groups (e.g. school proms, Berkeley Public Schools Fund annual
      fundraiser, family reunions, etc.). Reviving the venue may see similar increases
      in demand, since few large event spaces at affordable prices exist in Berkeley.
   ● If the pier were reopened under the baseline condition of a somewhat shorter
      2000’ without the ferry, an economic valuation study by Antinori et al. (2025)
      predicts almost double the visitation rate for fishing over future expected visits to
      the shoreline along Seawall Drive.

COB 2021 states that replacement solely of the pier with a 2000-foot pier would cost
$26 million, and to 3000 feet, around $32-$44 million. City staff have indicated in public
meetings that COB’s share of the waterside costs alone for the pier/ferry project would
be approximately $26 million. Therefore, a 2000-foot reopened pier facility alone,
without a ferry, is a reasonable baseline, as it is the opportunity cost of the WETA/COB
pier/ferry project. Even as WETA and COB update all cost estimates to 2025 dollars
across all projects, as long as all costs scale relatively equally, this fact would remain.

   3. Impact on recreational and subsistence fishing

Before its closure in 2015, the pier was one of the most popular fishing venues in the
Bay Area. It hosted fishing derbies like the “mudfish” and kids’ fishing derbies. Closure
eliminated or displaced this activity to other piers. Fishers continue to visit the area
along the shoreline (which requires a license) as opposed to pier fishing, which does
not. According to a recent survey analysis from 2021 to 2023 (Antinori et al. 2025), 77%
of a random sample of shoreline anglers fished in Berkeley prior to COVID, implying a
long association with the area, and 61% said they prefer to fish in Berkeley in
comparison to other locations. In addition, 55% reported fishing for consumption needs.
Other locations like the piers in Pinole and Antioch are not substitutes for fishing at
Berkeley but appear to be complementary assets, with fishers traveling north and south
along I-80 (Antinori et al., 2025, p. 23) to these fishing spots.

Because pier fishing does not require a license, it is especially attractive to low-income
anglers. For example, Quimby et al. (2020) reported that in a study of pier fishing in
generally wealthy Santa Barbara, California, 88% reported annual household income
below the state median and 73% were non-white. Further, the study of Los Angeles pier
fishing by Stevenson et al. (2012) found that the majority identified as persons of color,
fished only from piers, and spoke English as a second language. Likewise, many
anglers who continue to fish the Berkeley shoreline are lower- to middle-income
(Antinori et al., 2025). This same study also projects an increase in angling visits should
the Berkeley pier reopen to even just a length of 2000’ without the ferry, of 70%, 88%
and 114% for low, middle, and high income anglers, respectively.

The environmental economics literature is replete with studies that estimate the value of
angling trips for recreational fishing. For example, Pendleton (2011) estimated a value

towards the high end (Pendleton and Rooke, 2006). California is second only to Florida
in percentage of population that participates in marine recreation (Pendleton and
Rooke, 2006). A recent study based on shoreline fishing at Berkeley puts the value at

their home to the Berkeley waterfront solely to fish. In springtime, sportfishing boats
and private boats go out into the Bay inside the Golden Gate bridge to fish for halibut.
According to a Barrientos et al. (2017) report on recreational benefits for the California
halibut recreational fishery, based on party/charter/private/rental fishing, Berkeley is the
highest valued port in northern California and most frequent port of embarkation for
halibut fishing for all of California.

   4. Fish habitat

While the comments in this section overlap with the section above, we attempt to isolate
and expand on impacts the biological resources themselves. The bay waters around
the pier prior to 2015 are known as spawning grounds for some fish species.
Fish populations are also migratory and seasonal, and interact with other parts of the
bay ecosystem. A number of plan elements could negatively affect fish habitat in the
area:
   ● Dredging to accommodate a large vessel alongside the pier close to shore
       (within 500’) plus initial construction and ongoing maintenance could interfere
       with all fish populations.
   ● An estimated 16 morning and 12 afternoon weekday crossings between Berkeley
       and San Francisco alone, plus 14 crossings on weekends and additional
       crossings for the Berkeley-Larkspur route, would create additional turbulence and
       noise, interfering with fish populations as well as angling (COB 2021 Feasibility
       Study).
   ● The original WETA plan called for a diesel ferry, while electric ferries were
       mentioned later in the process. Uncertainty remains whether an electric ferry


                                                                                          9
      would accommodate the natural conditions of the Berkeley pier, which
      experiences high winds and waves during much of the year.

EIR scoping input: EIR should assess impacts on all fish and marine species that are
part of the ecosystem in the path of the proposed WETA ferry with respect to:
   ● New and long-term maintenance dredging
   ● Siltation from a sheet pile breakwater that interferes with halibut and other fish
        spawning grounds and habitat
   ● Turbidity/turbulence and noise from the San Francisco, Larkspur and other
        routes in the plan
   ● Ship strikes
   ● Light pollution
   ● Diesel versus electric vessels

      6. Characterization of alternatives

          a. Location alternatives must be adequately assessed

The only alternative examined by the WETA ferry plan is a site innermost in the
Berkeley Marina near an old slab of concrete that once served as a ferry dock (aka the
Old Ferry Pier). The last time this was used for a ferry was in 1989 by the Red and
White Fleet for about six months after the earthquake, after which it suspended service
due to lack of ridership. This location is not viable as an alternative, and acts a
“straw man” in the analysis as currently structured. During public meetings, the city
only offered as alternatives differences in breakwater design of the project.

Several feasible alternatives exist which have not been analyzed. One is the
previous D&E dock location near the Hornblowers docks and the Doubletree Hotel.
Another is the Hornblower docks themselves, in underused space. Third are docks
currently used by the Genentech and previously the Tidelines ferries, which closed
during the pandemic. The latter is a central location in the Marina and adds only two
minutes’ travel time to San Francisco, based on the Genentech ferry
departures/arrivals, excluding docking maneuver time.

In addition, an in-harbor dock reduces need for new dredging, and thus marginal
environmental impact, as the Marina undergoes maintenance dredging regardless. As
such it possibly reduces Berkeley’s cost of Marina maintenance because the needed
dredging costs could be shared over the years with WETA, and thus provide a more
viable economic pathway for ferry service.

EIR scoping input:
   ● EIR scoping should integrate the ferry project with the WSP and include
      examination of options to access a central in-Marina facility.
   ● Specifically, the EIR must assess, as a docking location alternative, a central
      location within the Marina, such as the previous D&E docks, Hornblower docks or
      the K docks.

          b. Alternative scale of ferry should be included
Current ridership demand estimates for the WETA Berkeley-SF are 4000, using a 400-
passenger ferry, according to WETA business plan documents. Several indicators
suggest the WETA ferry may not reach this level of usage. Public transit demand is
down across the Bay Area systems including BART. About 60% of respondents in a
COB questionnaire said they would never use the ferry to commute. For leisure, 56%
said they’d never use it or use it less than 10 trips annually. There is a real danger
that the envisioned large-scale ferry with about 28 crossings per day would be
mainly empty. Stranded assets would pose a considerable cost to WETA and
Berkeley, as well as having serious environmental impacts.

A reasonable alternative of a smaller scale ferry service must be included in the EIR. A
smaller ferry would result in less cost, should demand predictions collapse. Smaller
scale service (and e.g. docking within the Marina) could reduce the financial burden on
Berkeley and at the same time fit demand patterns more accurately. However, smaller
scale ferries have not been considered in planning as alternatives.

       7. Impact of tree removals

The Marina area faces the mouth of the Golden Gate, referred to as “The Slot” (see graphic
below). Existing large trees provide valuable wind relief to recreational visitors in the park for
land-based activities like picnics and park events. They also shelter half of the South Sailing
Basin, which supports children’s water sports training. Therefore, removing large trees should
be evaluated in terms of potentially decreasing the value of the park for recreational visitors, as
well as air quality and wildlife habitat. For example, close to the pier, large trees are the
roosting location for the resident wild turkeys. Hummingbirds nest in trees in Shorebird park.
The white-tailed kite and osprey hunt in Shorebird Park and south cove waters.

EIR scope input: Include analysis of tree removal impact on aesthetics, useability,
wind protection and bird and animal habitat in the area.

      8. Ferry parking management

While this section overlaps with our first section on the waterfront as affordable
public access and cultural resource, here we emphasize the particular impact of
ferry patron parking on the Berkeley Marina as a major concern for equity,
recreational, and land use. The parking study introduces paid parking (currently free)
to recreational users. It forces them to park farther from Marina parks and shorelines.
For many, visits to the project area and waterfront are intimately tied to access via
private vehicle as well as public transportation. Sailors, windsurfers, wingers,
kayakers, fishers, families, guardians with children at the playgrounds, dog
walkers, picnickers and others, crucially, cannot pile considerable amounts of
gear, children, or animals aboard the 51 bus. Thus, public parking remains critical
to many who visit. A total of 16% in the Kittleson intercept survey said they
cancelled trips to the Marina because they could not find parking.




                                                                                      16
Bus service to support ferry ridership is mentioned in the ferry parking study. However,
consultants noted the limited utility of buses for ferry patrons. The Kittleson study notes
in its case studies that AC Transit discontinued shuttle service to the Seaplane Lagoon
WETA terminal due to low ridership, and that AC transit service to the Harbor Bay ferry
terminal currently has few users.

It is unclear if and how Berkeley officials will fulfill their statutory obligation to keep ferry
riders from usurping recreational users’ parking. Past experience with Tidelines and
Genentech ferries shows that the city did not succeed in getting ferry patrons to
park outside of the South Cove recreational parking lots, raising doubt as to the
city’s motivation and/or ability to meet its BCDC obligations to preserve
recreational parking and access.

The recent parking study addendum also calls for expanding ferry patron parking to
Marina Boulevard and the Skates parking lot. This balloons the scope of the project
location as noted in the NOP. This proposal overlaps with the COB Waterfront Specific
Plan, which has not been completed, and is compiled independently from the pier/ferry
project even though the pier has historically been the central feature of the Berkeley
waterfront.

The 315-space Seawall Drive lot was closed in 2018 to deal with a homeless RV
encampment, and has been mostly closed ever since. This was a major lapse in the
city’s responsibility to maintain public access. Now the Berkeley Water
Transportation Pier Ferry Project intends to devote the entire Seawall Drive lot to ferry
parking. This will usurp Seawall Drive parking for people who fish or walk on the pier,
which is more than ironic, given that this expensive project was presented as a way to
reestablish the pier. Also usurped will be parking for new facilities at Hs Lordships
restaurant site, parking to serve the southern Shorebird Cove picnic sites, and the
boardsailing and swimming launch at Hs Lordships Cove, to say nothing of overflow
parking for the heavily impacted South Cove parking lots.

   ● Expand project location to include all locations noted in the addendum to the
     parking study.

       9. Bird nesting and feeding grounds

The waterfront area is home to many birds in Cesar Chavez Park
(https://ebird.org/hotspot/L285281/bird-list) and the Sylvia McLaughlin State Park, part
of the Eastshore State Park system. Resident bird populations include ospreys who
feed in nearby waters.

       10. Clarify economic factors to contextualize project feasibility

As BCDC considers environmental impacts overall and weighs any opportunity cost for
not building the large-scale WETA ferry, realistic information should be provided about
those opportunity costs. WETA documents offer no predictions, nor conjectures, of
secondary economic benefits for Berkeley. Consultant reports suggest minimal
economic benefits. The Keyser Marston report suggests very modest contributions to
the Marina Fund which may generate only up to 10% more of any revenues the city
receives from food and beverage consumer spending (Keyser Marston, 2021, pp. 8-9).
The report extends only aspirational comments, e.g. “It is hoped that a large-scale
ferry with weekend service will attract an additional hotel” (Keyser Marston, p. 9).
The WETA ferry terminal at Jack London Square, Oakland, has failed to prevent a
large vacancy rate in commercial rental space. Oakland’s Waterfront Hotel which
closed in January 2025 had 145 rooms, just steps away from this terminal.

EIR scoping should include: Currently available estimates of the secondary economic
benefits so that reviewers may weigh the proposed project against its environmental
and human costs. The scoping should compare this against the baseline of 2000’ pier
without ferry.

       11. Compatibility of electrification and emergency service

The waterfront area is land filled for recreational purposes. As fill, the waterfront area
servicing the dock may not be stable in an earthquake. The ferry dock is described as
serving an electric ferry if feasible. In such a case, an electrification facility to serve
electric zero-emission ferry vessels may not withstand natural disasters such as
earthquakes, tsunamis or violent storms.

Storms also come from north and south throughout the year, making navigation in “The
Slot” through the Golden Gate (see above) more challenging. Any final recharging
station and breakwater design compatible with supporting electric ferry service year-
round and in emergencies should be confirmed within the EIR. With this information,
environmental impacts noted above (e.g. dredging, aesthetics, recreation, habitat) can
be more clearly assessed.

FEASIBILITY

BCDC is responsible for providing a critical review of developer proposals on the shoreline,
and in this case, on bay ﬁll intended for recreation. As I have stated, the city proposes to
diminish public access. No improvements to the existing launch are proposed, parking
fees will reduce use, and the proposal includes only 550 feet of recreational pier suitable
for ﬁshing, to replace the 3,000-foot-long ﬁshing pier. Neither the City nor WETA have funds
to build a Berkeley terminal, or for operation of a terminal if built. The city has not updated
the cost estimates for many years, and the original cost estimated that Berkeley would pay
for all the recreational facilities at a cost then estimated to be $29 million. WETA has
acknowledged in their regular meeting packets that they don’t have enough money to build
a new capital facility estimated to cost over $120 million or continue to cover the
operational subsidies required to run the existing system after 2030. WETA adopted a
budget for $81 million in operational costs and expects to carry 3,032,823 passengers.
That works out to be a cost per passenger—each direction—of $26.70, and a subsidy of
$22 per passenger in each direction. A roundtrip on the existing system thus requires a $44
subsidy. Such an approach does not remotely achieve the established goal of collecting
40% of operational costs from the farebox, and is not sustainable for the existing system,
much less an expanded system. It is premature to evaluate whether the access measures
proposed by Berkeley are the maximum feasible consistent with the project.

ZERO EMISSIONS?

WETA has begun to acquire electric ferries; two 400 passenger ferries for the
Oakland/Alameda to San Francisco operation are on order as part of WETA’s Rapid Electric
Emission-Free (REEF) Program. It is not clear that funds will be available to continue that
effort. The WETA Board meeting of October 9, 2025, included a Memorandum on the Long-
Term RM3 Capital funding Outlook. On page 3, under Scenario A, “Focus on REEF” it stated
that if there was full implementation of the REEF program, “Project capital budgets for the
Berkeley and Redwood City expansion projects would not be fully funded.”

Ferry transport, even with electric ferries, is not a zero-emission program. Charging
electric batteries involves some level of carbon-based energy generation. Most passengers
arrive by car. According to the 2025 Onboard Ferry Survey, which asked how riders got to
                                               5


the terminal, 64% got there by car for the whole system. Some terminals have even higher
percentages of passengers arriving by car, Vallejo at 89%, Alameda Main Street at 74%,
Richmond at 86%, and Alameda Seaplane Lagoon at 82% access by car. I have
commented in the scoping sessions for the preparation of an EIR for the ferry proposal that
the EIR must reveal the true energy footprint of a new terminal. That EIR has not yet been
issued in draft.

RECREATIONAL PIER

The exhibits for this DRB meeting include beautiful renderings of a new segment of
recreational pier, a plaza at the end of University Avenue that is derivative of the San
Francisco Ferry Plaza, and a new cycle track that parallels the Bay Trail. These facilities,
and the parking management proposal that the city presented to the DRB earlier, seem to
be much more expensive than prior design ideas—but no revised cost estimates are
provided.

It seems inconsistent with the permit history of this site and the adopted plan to devote
substantial resources to a new separated bicycle path less than 400 feet from the existing,
separated Bay Trail and yet not carry out access improvements included in the existing
speciﬁc plan. While the design for the new recreational portion of the pier is attractive,
there is nothing that we have seen that compares its utility to that of the existing 3,000-foot
pier for ﬁshing and for strolling out onto the Bay. As pointed out earlier, WETA does not
appear to be willing to fund any of the recreational elements of their proposed commuter
terminal, and the City of Berkeley has no funds either.

LATENT DEMAND

We have repeatedly raised the concern that the city is dramatically underestimating the
latent demand for access to the shoreline. The closure of the pier and the lack of general
maintenance of the Marina has clearly resulted in dramatically lower visitation to the
Waterfront Park. If these facilities had been maintained, we would see an even greater
demand for parking and access to our shoreline. We have a clear case study of latent local
demand for shoreline access in the Albany Beach Bay Trail project, where improvements to
shoreline access drew new users. The city, however, has ignored this case study and its
implications for Berkeley.

CONCLUSION

At the last meeting with the DRB, the Board members speciﬁcally asked the city to engage
existing users—who provide about 2/3 of the revenue for the Marina Fund to understand
use patterns and incorporate those ﬁndings into its plans. We are disappointed that the
city has not followed this direction. The city essentially presents the DRB with the same
narrative, ignoring the information about visitors that we have provided to BCDC staff. The
city fails to recognize the value that the marina offers to its residents, and those of the

region—unique and easy access to our shoreline and Bay. A way to get close to the Bay, get
out on the Bay on recreational equipment or a recreational pier, or simply enjoy the wildlife
that can be found. It is important to note that recreational users are not opposed to either
a ferry or more commercial development in the marina. We are, however, opposed to
designs that profoundly compromise existing access, and economic assumptions that are
largely untested and hidden. Again, we ask the city to engage in a constructive and open
conversation with existing users to craft solutions that are sustainable and are consistent
with the purposes for which BCDC allowed this part of the Bay to be ﬁlled.
